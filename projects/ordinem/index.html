ORDINEM

PvP Trading Card Game · PC (Epic Games) · 2022–2026 · Lead Game Designer

ORDINEM is a PvP Trading Card Game Battler in the vein of Magic: The Gathering, Hearthstone, and Legends of Runeterra. You command an entire City, drawing on its Orders of weaves and threads to summon followers that bear down on your opponent's City.

What I did

  • Lead Game Designer. Designed every game mechanic and system (turn phases, player actions, card types, costs, resources) and all ~210 cards in the first set.
  • Vision holder. Created the theming, lore and art style, and kept the whole team aligned to them through weekly meetings, Discord and Monday (our task manager).
  • Balance and feedback. Sole owner of card balance. Triaged feedback from players and devs, and vetted and prioritised QA bugs.
  • Documentation. Wrote and maintained the Game Design Document (Google Docs from Jan 2023, then GitBook, through early 2026). Built the card spreadsheet, the single source of truth for every card's numbers, and maintained it from Jan 2023 to early 2026 as player feedback, team feedback, technical limits and my own playtesting came in.
  • Research. Played and studied MTG Arena, Hearthstone, Legends of Runeterra, Riftbound, Gods Unchained, Eternal and more, to find references, explain concepts and validate ideas.
  • Testing. Constantly testing and collating feedback from myself, other founders and players to improve the game.
  • Hands-on polish. For v1.5 I implemented visual, UI, animation and sound-feedback improvements myself in Unity.

Design overview: why ORDINEM isn't just MTG

My brief was: "Make a similarly complex game to MTG so we can capture that market, but in crypto." So I wanted to keep the MTG mechanics that give it depth, the ones few other digital card games use, while still giving players a reason to pick ORDINEM over MTG Arena.

My research showed that almost every competitor sets itself apart by changing mana or lands. I didn't want to push those too far, or we'd lose the "MTG-ness" that was the whole point.

So I flipped the perspective instead. In MTG you're a single Planeswalker. In ORDINEM you're an entire City. The main difference comes from the theme, lore and win conditions rather than the core rules. I then designed mechanics to support that shift and to fix some of the problems MTG Arena's best-of-one format is known for, such as having no sideboard (no 'flexible' card slots to give a nice/horrible matchup a fighting chance). The results were City abilities (inspired by Hearthstone's hero powers) and an Archive (inspired by Eternal's Market).

The Market and the full City design were never fully implemented because other work took priority, but Problem 3 below shows where the City design was heading.

Problem 1

Making lands feel good

The problem: In MTG, drawing too many or too few lands can decide a game by pure luck. Card games have been trying to fix this for decades. I wanted a system that balanced deckbuilding expression, meaningful choices during play, and being easy to understand.

The iterations
  1. Weaves in the main deck, like MTG. With how our thread system worked, this was far too close to MTG, so it was scrapped.
  2. A separate weave deck, with a free weave each turn on top of your draw. That's essentially Hearthstone or Legends of Runeterra. Those games make cheap cards stronger since you're guaranteed your resource each turn, and our cards weren't designed that way. It felt refreshing in testing, but I really wanted this to be a tactical decision, so I didn't go with it.
  3. Each turn, choose: draw a card or enweave. Much better for both feel and balance, but something was still missing. The choice didn't feel meaningful, why would you not just enweave until you could play everything in your hand?
  4. Double every cost and give two 'expands' per turn (draw + draw, draw + enweave, or enweave + enweave). Players disliked it at first but grew to like it. Two problems remained: double-enweaving was always correct until turn 3 or 4, and doing maths with doubled numbers in your head was a pain.
  5. Back to one deck, like V1, but players by default draw two cards a turn. My final design, when I realised even with all my theorising and testing there is amybe a reason MTG is as popular as it is - and the randomness of lands contributes to that. All we need to do is not make AS likely to be completely mana screwed/flooded. Designed for the next update before development was paused.

V5 was designed but never implemented, due to game pause. The live game uses V4.

I also explored and paper-tested many other systems: "any card can be played as a land" (Duel Masters, Star Wars: Unlimited), dropped because of the awful 'but I want to play this card!' feeling; and a Riftbound-style system, where lands can be 'recycled' and shuffled back into the deck, which would at least give two separate decks a reason to exist.

Takeaway Sometimes simpler is better, but you still have to test things to find out what's fun and practical.

Problem 2

The UX of using mana

The problem: MTG-style coloured mana was hard enough to design around (see the design overview), and on a digital client it became a huge UX problem: players struggled to pay with the right colours.

The iterations
  1. Straight MTG mana. Tap weaves for the Orders of thread they produce, either when you need them or automatically. MTG Arena makes this look easy, but after only a little work on it I realised the UI behind it was far beyond our team's size and budget.
  2. Hybrid threads that count as more than one Order. How do you show hybrid colours clearly, and let players spend the colour they meant to? We actually built the UI, and only then saw how convoluted and messy it was.
  3. Weaves add threads to your pool automatically each turn. That raised a new question: pick the colour every time, or lock it in when the weave is played? Picking every turn became a chore as weaves piled up, so we switched to locking it in. This was better, but players kept locking in the wrong Order and regretting it.
  4. Influence, (borrowed from Eternal.) Threads become Orderless (MTG 'generic' or 'colourless'), and weaves give 'Influence'' instead, which unlocks cards of their Order. The trade-off is that once you meet a card's Influence requirement, you can cast as many of them as you want so long as you have the threads (in MTG every cast needs its 'coloured pips'), butttttt it's far simpler to use and far easier to show in a digital UI.

V4 was designed but never implemented, due to a team miscommunication and then the pause. The live game uses V3.

Takeaway Whether players can see and use something effectively in the UI is a design constraint, not an afterthought.

Problem 3

Making the player feel like a City

The problem: The player is meant to command an entire City, but neither the mechanics nor the flavour supported that, so players never felt like one.

The iterations
  1. One activated ability per City, paid with threads. Too simple, and too close to Hearthstone's hero powers. The choice wasn't interesting.
  2. The ability plus a change to starting life, to offset the wilder abilities. More interesting, but not there yet.
  3. Cities change several starting stats (life, starting cards and starting weaves) on top of the ability. More intriguing, and it gave me extra levers for balance, but it still felt lacking.
  4. Each City has a quest that powers you up as you complete it, or eventually wins you the game.

V3 is live. V4 was fully designed but not implemented, as development was paused.

Also explored before settling on quests: how the WoW TCG and Heroes of the Storm handle quests; buildable structures that give bonuses but double as objectives to attack (like MTG planeswalkers); places you capture and hold (like MTG Battles or Riftbound's battlefields).

Takeaway Stat tweaks can balance a City, but they can't make you feel like one. The fantasy has to come from what you do.

Problem 4

Making each Order's mechanics work

The problem: I wanted each of the five Orders to have its own varied, useful set of keywords and effects that also came out of the lore. Some cards went through a dozen iterations.

The iterations
  1. Lots of unique, flavourful mechanics per Order. There were almost 20 keywords (e.g. Decay for Necros, our "black"), and many cards had several. They were fun to design, but hard to program, hard for players to learn, and spread too thin: each archetype had too few cards to actually work as a deck.
  2. A big prune. I simplified and streamlined the cards. But cutting that much power and complexity left some archetypes under-supported, and the surviving mechanics weren't memorable, partly from technical limits and partly because some just weren't fun in practice.
  3. Back to the roots. For v1.0 I reworked every Order's mechanics and many of its cards around a small set of signature mechanics (from over 20 keywords in total to under 10), with enough cards behind each to carry its archetypes. v1.5 added balance passes for playability and fun.

Takeaway Complexity is fun for the designer, but it comes with a real cost to development and to players learning your game. Complexity has to be earnt and needed.

Problem 5

Naming things

The problem: Honestly, the hardest part of the job for me. Every name had to tell most players what it does, be flavourful, and not copy another popular game's term. That's... really hard.

I'm still not happy with a lot of the names! Most mechanics, card types, actions and phases were renamed several times. The breakthrough was to stop hunting for a middle ground and lean fully into the lore of 'cities at war':
